\section{Introduction}
In this assignment, we implemented two different Bayesian trackers: the Kalman filter tracker 
and the particle filter tracker. We derived the input matrices for the Random Walk, 
Nearly Constant Velocity, and Nearly Constant Acceleration motion models. The Kalman
 filter tracker was tested on three artificial trajectories, while the particle filter 
 tracker was evaluated on the VOT2014 dataset. For the particle filter tracker, we compared the performance of different
  motion models and determined the optimal dynamic noise parameter for all three models.
   Additionally, we tested the tracker with varying numbers of particles and explored its
    performance across different color spaces.


\section{Experiments}
\subsection{Kalman filtering}
In this section, we derive the input matrices (see Appendix) for the Random Walk (RW), 
Nearly Constant Velocity (NCV), and Nearly Constant Acceleration (NCA) motion models. 
These models were tested on three different trajectories to evaluate their performance.


Figures~\ref{fig:kf1},~\ref{fig:kf2}, and~\ref{fig:kf3} show the trajectories and their 
filtered counterparts for the different motion models, as well as the effects of varying the 
dynamics noise constant (\(q\)) and the measurement covariance constant(\(r\)). In all three plots,
 we observe that when \(q\) is large and \(r\) is small, the filtered measurements closely 
 follow the actual measurements. This is expected because a larger \(q\) assigns more uncertainty 
 to the dynamics, leading the tracker to rely more heavily on the measurements for prediction.


 On the other hand, when \(r\) is set high and \(q\) is set low, the tracker 
 relies more on the motion model. This is evident because, in this case, the 
 random walk struggles to follow the path quickly enough. For the NCV and NCA models, 
 we observe that the paths start to deviate significantly from the measurements, particularly
  in the second plot. However, this is in general not a negative outcome. In scenarios where
   measurements are unreliable or sparse, the tracker can perform better by depending more on
    the motion model, which compensates for the poor measurements.


    The optimal parameters depend on both the choice of motion model and the specific
     application. For the Random Walk model, it is advisable to rely more
      on the measurements, particularly when the movements are erratic.
       In such cases, values like \(q = 5\) and \(r = 1\) would be suitable. 
       In contrast, for the Nearly Constant Velocity  and Nearly Constant Acceleration
        models, where the movement is more predictable or dynamic, we can place more
         trust in the motion model, which means selecting a larger value for \(r\).

\subsection{Results of the basic particle filter tracker implementation}
In this section, we implemented the particle filter tracker using the Nearly
 Constant Velocity  motion model and color histograms for the visual model. The
  results for the entire VOT 2014 dataset are presented in Table~\ref{tab:pf_tracker_results}.
   For the implementation, we set the update speed to \(\alpha = 0.0001\), the kernel size 
   to \(\sigma = 1\), and used 16 bins in the histogram. The dynamic noise constant was set 
   to \(0.8\) of the size of the smaller dimension of the patch, and the number of particles 
   was set to 100. Additionally, we used the Epanechnikov kernel on the patch before histogram 
   extraction and employed the Hellinger distance to compute the similarity between histograms.
    Furthermore, we ensured that particle positions were clipped if they were assigned a location
     outside of the image.

\subsection{Testing using different motion models}
We tested the tracker using all three of the aforementioned motion models. For each model,
 we experimented with different values for the dynamic noise constant \(q\). Note that here \(q\) 
 represents the ratio of the actual dynamics noise constant to the smaller
   dimension of the patch.

   The number of failures and average overlap for each motion model and different 
   values of \(q\) are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:motion_models}. It is evident that 
   the RW and NCV models significantly outperform
    the NCA model, which struggles in both the number of
     failures and average overlap. The RW and NCV both manage to reach around 30 failures and 
     and average overlap at around 0.5.

     We can see that for the RW and NCV models, a \( q \) value around 1 works best,
      while for the NCA model, a smaller \( q \) is preferable (Note that while the average overlap 
      is larger at larger q, that is the consequence of the tracker being reset more times). This is because assigning a 
      large uncertainty to the RW and NCV models helps the tracker rely more on the measurements,
       which is beneficial for these simpler motion models. In contrast, for the NCA model, a 
       larger \( q \) increases the uncertainty in the acceleration, making the particle positions
        more erratic. This leads to many particles being pushed outside the target area, resulting 
        in a higher number of failures. Therefore, a smaller \( q \) is more effective in the NCA
         model, as it reduces this unpredictability and keeps the particles within a more controlled
          range.

\subsection{Testing the influence of number of particles}
We also tested the dependence of performance on the number of particles (using the NCV 
motion model).
 In Figure~\ref{fig:particles}, we show the average overlap, total failures, 
 and average speed as functions of the number of particles. We observe that with 
 a small number of particles, the tracker's performance drops significantly. However,
  after reaching approximately 30 to 50 particles, further increases in the number do
   not result in significant improvements in performance. On the other hand, the average
    speed decreases at a rate proportional to \(1 \times 10^{-1}\), as the computation time
     increases linearly with the number of particles.

\section{Conclusion}
We found that when using the Kalman filter, the dynamic noise constant should be lower for 
the Random Walk (RW) motion model compared to the Nearly Constant Velocity (NCV) and Nearly
 Constant Acceleration (NCA) models. This is because the RW model lacks flexibility and should
  rely more on the measurements.

The particle filter tracker performed well on the dataset. We observed that the RW and NCV models 
significantly outperformed the NCA model. For the RW and NCV models, an optimal dynamic noise constant 
\(q \approx 1\) was determined, while the NCA model performed better with \(q \approx 0.2\).

We also found that increasing the number of particles improved the tracker's performance. However,
 after approximately 40 particles, the improvements stagnated, while the computation speed continued 
 to decrease.

Furthermore, we determined that the BGR and RGB color spaces outperformed other color spaces in terms 
of tracker performance.

Here we present the matrices derived for the motion models.
\subsection{Random walk}
\[
\mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}, \quad
\mathbf{F} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
\boldsymbol{\phi} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
\mathbf{L} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
\mathbf{Q} = \begin{bmatrix} \Delta t\,q & 0 \\ 0 & \Delta t\,q \end{bmatrix}, \quad
\mathbf{H} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\]
